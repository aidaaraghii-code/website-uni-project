Persepolis

The ancient ceremonial capital of the Achaemenid Empire.

Overview

The city of columns and gates

Persepolis (in Persian, Takht-e Jamshid) was built in the sixth century BC by Darius the Great as the ceremonial capital of the Achaemenid Empire. It was the place where the king received delegates from across his enormous realm — from Egypt to India — as they gathered to pay tribute.

The city stands on a huge stone platform raised above the Marvdasht plain, near today's city of Shiraz. Although Alexander the Great's army burned it in 330 BC, its tall columns, wide stairways and carved stone reliefs survive remarkably well.

History

Built over three reigns

Construction began around 518 BC under Darius the Great. His son Xerxes and grandson Artaxerxes continued adding palaces and halls for roughly sixty years — which is why the platform holds several separate buildings, each with its own grand name.

In 330 BC Alexander the Great's soldiers set fire to the city. Charred wood and soot marks are still visible on some walls today. Historians still debate whether the burning was accidental or deliberate — but the ruins survived, were rediscovered by European travellers in the 16th century, and have been studied by archaeologists ever since.

What to see

Key features of the site

  • The Grand Stairway — wide, low steps carved so guests could climb them even while riding.
  • The Gate of All Nations — an entrance guarded by colossal stone bulls.
  • The Apadana Palace — the great audience hall whose roof once rested on 36 slender columns.
  • Carved reliefs — detailed stone pictures of delegates from 23 nations bringing gifts.
  • The Hall of One Hundred Columns — the largest hall ever built by the Achaemenids.
  • Royal tombs — graves of later kings carved into the cliff behind the platform.

Key facts

  • Location: Marvdasht, about 60 km northeast of Shiraz
  • Founded: around 518 BC by Darius the Great
  • Empire: Achaemenid (Persian)
  • Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1979
  • Famous for: pillar halls and carved reliefs
