Isfahan & Naqsh-e Jahan

The historic square of "Half the World" and its great mosques.

Overview

The square that built a city

Isfahan is called Nesf-e Jahan — "half the world" — and its heart is Naqsh-e Jahan Square (also called Imam Square). Built in the early 1600s by the Safavid king Shah Abbas I, the square was laid out as a grand public space where polo was once played at the very center.

Around the square sit some of Iran's most famous buildings: the blue-tiled Imam Mosque, the jewel-like Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, the Ali Qapu palace gate and the Grand Bazaar. Since 1979 the square has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The two mosques

Imam Mosque

The Imam Mosque (formerly Shah Mosque) is the largest building on the square. It was finished in 1629 and is famous for its massive turquoise dome, its twin minarets and walls covered in thousands of glazed tiles. Visitors notice that its entrance arch is angled toward the square while the prayer hall itself points exactly to Mecca — a clever piece of orientation that is easily explained in the mosque's vestibule.

Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque

Across the square sits the smaller Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, built as a private royal prayer hall between 1603 and 1619. It has no minarets and no courtyard; instead its charm comes from the warm cream and peach tones of its dome, and from a peacock's tail of tiles that appears in the entrance hall when the light hits it just right. It is widely considered the most beautiful interior in Persian architecture.

What to see

Around the square

  • Ali Qapu Palace — the royal gate with its famous music room on the top floor.
  • Chehel Sotoun — a palace of "forty pillars" with mirrored reflections in its pool.
  • Sheikh Lotfollah's colour change — the dome shifts from cream to pink to gold through the day.
  • The Grand Bazaar — arcades that lead out from the square into the old city.
  • Bridges across the Zayandeh River — the 33-arch Si-o-se-pol, especially at dusk.

Key facts

  • Location: Central Isfahan, Iran
  • Built: early 1600s under Shah Abbas I (Safavid dynasty)
  • Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1979
  • Square size: about 500 x 160 metres, a former polo ground
  • Famous for: tile work and the two great mosques
